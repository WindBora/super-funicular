Conference presentation script — approximately 7 minutes

E-POLARIZED PLANE-WAVE SCATTERING BY A FINITE SINUSOIDAL PEC GRATING VIA THE NYSTRÖM METHOD

A. Petryshyn, S. V. Dukhopelnykov, and T. L. Zinenko
O. Y. Usikov Institute for Radiophysics and Electronics, NAS of Ukraine


SLIDE 1 — TITLE
Target time: 0:00–0:40

Good morning. I am Andrii Petryshyn, and this work was carried out with Serhii
Dukhopelnykov and Tetiana Zinenko at the O. Y. Usikov Institute for
Radiophysics and Electronics of the National Academy of Sciences of Ukraine.

This work was supported by the National Research Foundation of Ukraine via
project number 2025.07/0094.

We study two-dimensional E-polarized plane-wave scattering by a finite
sinusoidal perfectly conducting grating. The practical question is simple:
how can we compute this open-arc problem accurately while also interpreting
the pattern using familiar infinite-grating ideas?

I will show a compact Nyström formulation, its numerical validation, and what
it reveals about corrugation height and electrical size.


SLIDE 2 — A FINITE GRATING COMBINES PERIODIC SCATTERING WITH UNAVOIDABLE EDGE EFFECTS
Target time: 0:40–1:25

An infinite periodic grating is naturally described by Floquet harmonics with
discrete propagation directions. A finite grating is different. Truncation
introduces two endpoints, so the induced current has edge singularities and
the radiation contains an end-generated background. With only five periods,
the periodic response is also aperture-broadened.

Therefore, we should not speak of exact Floquet channels for the finite
object. Our central result is more careful: the infinite-grating harmonic
directions remain very useful reference markers for the broad lobes of the
finite-grating pattern, while the specular lobe and the edge contribution
remain visible.


SLIDE 3 — THREE DIMENSIONLESS PARAMETERS DEFINE THE SCATTERING PROBLEM
Target time: 1:25–2:15

The problem is two-dimensional because the structure is invariant along the z
axis, and E polarization is represented by the scalar component U equal to E
sub z.

The zero-thickness PEC strip is parameterized by Gamma of t equal to L t in x
and h cosine of pi P t in y, for t from minus one to one. The geometry is
therefore controlled by the number of periods P and the relative height h over
L. Frequency enters through kL.

The equations allow arbitrary incidence angle beta, although the numerical
results shown here use normal incidence, beta equal to pi over two. The
projected grating period is two L divided by P.


SLIDE 4 — EDGE EXTRACTION TURNS THE OPEN-ARC PROBLEM INTO A STABLE NYSTRÖM SYSTEM
Target time: 2:15–3:20

The numerical difficulty is concentrated at the two open ends. Instead of
asking the discretization to discover the singular current, we factor the
known inverse-square-root behavior analytically. The remaining density v is
smooth.

The original single-layer equation has a logarithmic singularity. We
differentiate it with respect to the observation parameter and explicitly
separate the Cauchy term, whose coefficient is minus two i over pi. The
remainder is continuous.

We then discretize the principal-value equation at interlaced first- and
second-kind Gauss–Chebyshev points. Differentiation loses an integration
constant, so one weighted supplementary equation restores equivalence to the
original integral equation. The result is a compact dense N-by-N system.


SLIDE 5 — INDEPENDENT CHECKS SUPPORT BOTH CONVERGENCE AND PHYSICAL ACCURACY
Target time: 3:20–4:20

Before interpreting the corrugated-grating patterns, we verified the solver in
two ways.

First, for P equal to five, h over L equal to zero point one, and kL equal to
twenty, the weighted L2 error decreases systematically with interpolation
order and reaches 2.81142 times ten to the minus five at N equal to 512.
Recomputing every unique publication case at N equal to 800 changes the
normalized total scattering cross section by at most 7.53315 times ten to the
minus six, and the full complex far-field pattern by at most 2.30664 times ten
to the minus five.

Second, for a flat strip, three independent implementations agree within
1.59298 times ten to the minus five. At kL equal to twenty, the normalized
cross section is 0.9999612, very close to the geometrical-optics limit.


SLIDE 6 — AT kL = 20, PERIODIC AND FINITE-APERTURE SIGNATURES COEXIST
Target time: 4:20–5:10

Now consider the representative five-period grating with h over L equal to
zero point one and kL equal to twenty under normal incidence.

In the total-field intensity, the incident and reflected waves interfere below
the grating and form a standing-wave pattern, while the opposite side shows a
shadow. In the far field, the first-order periodic-reference harmonics are
propagating, and the finite grating develops broad off-normal lobes near their
directions.

But the pattern is not the pattern of an infinite surface. The specular
reflection lobe remains, and the endpoints generate a background across angle.
This coexistence is the main physical signature of a short periodic aperture.


SLIDE 7 — CORRUGATION REDISTRIBUTES THE PATTERN MORE THAN THE TOTAL SCATTERED POWER
Target time: 5:10–6:00

At fixed P equal to five and kL equal to twenty, increasing the corrugation
height changes both the phase and magnitude of the induced current along each
period. The angular pattern is therefore redistributed between the specular
and off-normal lobes.

The integrated power changes much less dramatically: the normalized total
scattering cross section moves from 0.9999612 for the flat strip to 0.9948396
at h over L equal to zero point zero five and 0.9926829 at h over L equal to
zero point one.

A presentation detail matters here: all polar plots in the paper use the same
absolute scale from minus thirty to plus fifteen decibels. If each pattern were
normalized to its own maximum, the real level changes would be concealed.


SLIDE 8 — THE FIRST-ORDER REFERENCE LOBES SWITCH ON AT kL = πP
Target time: 6:00–6:55

Frequency changes both the electrical width and the set of propagating
reference harmonics. At normal incidence, the reference condition is cosine
phi sub m equal to m pi P over kL.

For P equal to five, the first-order threshold is kL equal to pi P, or
15.70796. Therefore, at kL equal to twelve, the plus and minus first orders are
evanescent. At sixteen, they have just become propagating, so the corresponding
finite-grating lobes appear near grazing. At twenty, those lobes move farther
away from grazing.

Their finite width comes from the short, five-period aperture and should narrow
as the number of periods increases. The normalized cross sections are
1.010974, 0.9968797, and 0.9926829 for the three cases.


SLIDE 9 — FLOQUET DIRECTIONS EXPLAIN THE LOBES; THE NYSTRÖM MODEL EXPLAINS THE FINITE GRATING
Target time: 6:55–7:40

To conclude, the differentiated Nyström formulation handles the physical edge
singularity explicitly and converges reliably with interpolation order. The
flat-strip comparison against analytical regularization and a pulse-basis
method of moments provides an independent numerical and physical check.

For the five-period sinusoidal grating, corrugation height primarily
redistributes the angular scattering, while frequency determines whether the
periodic-reference orders can propagate and where their lobes appear.

The infinite-grating Floquet directions therefore provide a useful
interpretive map, but the finite object retains broadening, specular reflection,
and edge-generated radiation. The key message is that both viewpoints are
necessary: periodic physics identifies the lobe directions, and the
finite-aperture solution determines the actual pattern.

Thank you. I welcome your questions.
